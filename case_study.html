Overview

The problem with every litter solution on the market

Cat owners go through the same journey. The litter box starts in the bathroom — out of sight, but litter ends up everywhere, so you buy a mat that barely helps. The mess becomes unbearable, so you invest in an enclosure. Now it looks cleaner, but the mat is still there, the litter trashcan is still sitting out in the open, and your setup still occupies a disproportionate footprint in your home.

Every enclosure on the market solves only one thing: hiding the litter box. None address where the waste goes, how litter gets tracked beyond the entrance, or what the product looks like in a real living room. Most are styled with cat-themed cutouts and barn-door silhouettes — decorative signals that announce rather than conceal their purpose.

"Imagine a piece of furniture you'd put in your hallway that also happened to contain everything cat litter–related — and you'd never know it."

This project set out to design exactly that.

The Challenge

Four problems, no complete solution

Existing products treat these as separate issues. This design treats them as one system.

Litter tracking

No mat or enclosure stops cats from carrying litter beyond the exit. The problem isn't containment — it's exit path geometry.

Exposed waste storage

Every enclosure hides the litter box. None account for the dedicated waste bin (Litter Genie, Litter Zero) that lives right next to it.

It doesn't look like furniture

Cat-themed cutouts and rustic barn styling signal their purpose immediately. No current product could blend into a modern living room or hallway.

Wasted real estate

A litter box, enclosure, mat, and waste bin together consume significant floor space with zero storage return for the owner.

Research & Discovery

Constraints worth engineering around

Before sketching anything, I established the non-negotiable dimensional and ergonomic requirements — for the cat, for common cleaning hardware, and for the space itself.

Feline ergonomics

Based on guidelines from International Cat Care and the American Association of Feline Practitioners, the enclosure's interior needed to meet minimum standards for a cat of any age or mobility level — not just a healthy adult.

Requirement Spec (in) Spec (cm) Rationale
Litter box headspace 15″ – 20″ 38 – 51 cm Full standing, turning, and periscoping
Step-over entry height 3″ – 5″ 7.5 – 12.5 cm Accessible for senior and mobility-limited cats
Tunnel / corridor diameter 8″ – 10″ 20 – 25 cm Neutral spine posture while walking through
Step rise (if stairs included) 5″ – 7″ 12.5 – 17.8 cm Reduces forelimb load for older cats on descent

Robot vacuum clearance

The base of the enclosure needed enough ground clearance to let a robot vacuum pass underneath — eliminating one of the most tedious cleaning tasks for cat owners. Two categories of robots required different clearances:

Robot type Required clearance Source
Standard (no LiDAR) 3.8″ – 4.0″ iRobot Roomba j/s series specs; RTINGS.com testing
LiDAR turret (Roborock, Dreame) 4.2″ – 4.5″ Roborock S8 Pro Ultra teardown; RTINGS.com

Key insight: Standard manufacturer height specs understate real-world clearance needs by 0.25–0.5″ due to suspension compression on rug transitions. The design targets 4.5″ ground clearance to cover both robot types without adjustment.

User insights

Reviewing community discussions and walkthroughs of how real cat owners manage their setups revealed two consistent patterns:

First, litter color matching between the box and enclosure interior is unnecessary — owners reported they would happily vacuum a contained corridor rather than find litter spread across their living space. The trade-off is obvious once named. Second, almost every setup — regardless of price point — follows the same layout: litter box, some form of concealment, a waste bin sitting openly adjacent, and a mat that only partially helps. The problem is systemic, not a matter of choosing the wrong product.

01

U-shaped maze corridor

The single most effective change was rethinking the entry path. A straight corridor lets a cat exit in one or two steps — meaning most litter leaves with them. A U-shaped internal maze creates three direction changes, dramatically increasing the number of steps taken inside the enclosure before the cat exits. More steps inside means more litter deposited on the interior floor before it ever reaches the outside world.

The trade-off is footprint. The U-shape adds width. But the calculus is clear: it is far preferable to vacuum a contained corridor than to find litter distributed across a living room floor.

02

Dedicated waste bin compartment

A separate interior compartment houses the litter waste bin — a Litter Genie, Litter Zero, or equivalent. This is the detail every competing product omits entirely. No enclosure on the market includes a solution for the waste container. Here, it is designed in from the start: enclosed, accessible from a discrete panel, and out of sight.

03

Usable top surface

The top of the enclosure is flat, clean, and load-bearing — designed for plants, books, a lamp, or any decorative items that make a surface feel intentional rather than incidental. An optional wire management cutout accommodates a lamp or charging cable without surface clutter. The enclosure earns its floor space by functioning as furniture in both directions.

Robot vacuum clearance

The base of the enclosure is raised to a minimum 4.5″ ground clearance — enough to accommodate both standard and LiDAR-equipped robot vacuums. This removes the need to manually clean under the unit and means the corridor floor — where tracked litter accumulates — is automatically cleaned on the robot's regular cycle.

Reflection

What this project taught me

The insight

The most important design decision wasn't aesthetic — it was the maze geometry. A straight path felt like the obvious choice for cat accessibility. Rethinking it as a litter-capture mechanism changed the entire interior layout. The constraint became the feature.

System thinking

Existing products were designed around a single pain point. Designing around the entire ecosystem — litter, waste, storage, cleaning tools, robot vacuums — produced a solution that no individual feature could have reached on its own.

The gap in the market

The research confirmed that no product in this category publishes a diagram showing where the waste bin goes. That omission isn't an oversight — it reflects a fundamental scoping decision that every existing product has made. Expanding that scope was the project.

If I had more time

A physical prototype would validate the maze corridor dimensions against real cats — specifically whether the U-turn geometry suits cats of varying size. I'd also explore a modular panel system so the enclosure could be specified in different widths for apartments of different sizes.
